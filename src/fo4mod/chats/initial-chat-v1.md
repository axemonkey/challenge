 # Westminster / As the World Burns

 ## The central premise

 There are really two stories running in parallel.

 The first is **Tommi Starr's story**, which we experience almost entirely through his pirate-radio broadcasts in 2077.

 The second is **Westminster's story**, which takes place in 2287, roughly 210 years later, and gradually becomes an investigation into his own identity, ancestry and the persistent feeling that something about him is fundamentally "wrong".

 The important thing is that Tommi's radio material is not merely comic relief or period flavour.

 It is the **voice of Westminster's past talking to him across two centuries**.

 Tommi is broadcasting into the night shortly before the end of the old world, completely unaware that his voice, his friends, his stupid jokes, his complaints, his philosophy and his ordinary life will eventually become part of the history of his descendant.

 Westminster, meanwhile, is living in the consequences of that vanished world without initially knowing what they are.

---

 # 1\. The chronology

 The current family chronology is:

 | Date | Event |
| --- | --- |
| **March 2077** | Tommi Starr and Abbey Westminster have Child A. |
| **September 2077** | Abbey and seven-month-old A are potentially in the United States, staying with the Veeks in Salem. |
| **October 2077** | The Great War. The player character enters Vault 111 with their spouse and Shaun. Abbey and A make their way into Vault 81. Tommi's fate in London is unknown. |
| **2112** | A, aged 35, has Child B in Vault 81. |
| **2150** | B, aged 38, has Child C in Vault 81. |
| **2180** | C, aged 30, has Child D. |
| **2215** | D, aged 35, has Child E. |
| **2247** | E, aged 42, has Westminster. |
| **2287** | Westminster is 40 and his story begins. |

The correction here is important: **Westminster is 40 in 2287**, not 30.

 That actually helps the character considerably.

 He isn't a young man stumbling into adulthood and wondering what he wants to be.

 He's a fully adult person who has already lived a substantial life and has gradually become convinced that something about himself is wrong.

 That makes his investigation more serious. He isn't simply trying to "find himself" in the conventional coming-of-age sense.

 He's trying to understand something that has bothered him for a long time.

---

 # 2\. Abbey and Vault 81

 I think Abbey and Child A entering Vault 81 is particularly strong.

 The image is simple:

 **March 2077:** Tommi and Abbey have a baby.

 **October 2077:** the world ends.

 Abbey has a seven-month-old child.

 Tommi is in London.

 Abbey and the baby somehow make it to America and eventually into Vault 81.

 From there, the family effectively disappears into history.

 That creates a very unusual ancestry for Westminster.

 He isn't descended from a famous wasteland hero.

 He is descended from:

 > **a woman who entered a Vault carrying an infant shortly before the world ended.**

 For the next two centuries, the family remains inside that closed community.

 Abbey becomes the beginning of an entire lineage.

 And Westminster eventually becomes the end of that particular historical chain — at least as far as he knows.

---

 # 3\. Tommi's fate should probably remain unknown for a while

 Rather than establishing immediately that Tommi died in London, it may be more useful to treat that as an unresolved question.

 The known information can simply be:

 > **October 2077: Abbey and A reach Vault 81. Tommi is in London. His fate is unknown.**

 That gives Westminster something to discover.

 Perhaps he assumes Tommi died.

 Perhaps he finds evidence suggesting Tommi survived.

 Perhaps he finds contradictory evidence.

 Perhaps the eventual truth is heroic.

 Perhaps it is mundane.

 Perhaps it is tragic.

 Perhaps Tommi simply died doing something extremely Tommi-like.

 The important thing is that **uncertainty itself becomes part of the family history**.

 And I would resist making Tommi secretly into some legendary post-apocalyptic badass. The more interesting possibility is that he remains Tommi: an ordinary, funny, flawed, sometimes irresponsible human being caught in an extraordinary event.

---

 # 4\. Tommi's radio show becomes an historical artefact

 This is one of the strongest ideas in the material.

 Tommi spends the broadcasts talking about:

 - cats;
- landlords;
- his flat;
- his friends;
- coffee;
- getting drunk;
- music;
- his dad;
- his granddad;
- his neighbours;
- his jobs;
- London;
- the weather;
- his mental state;
- stupid things he has done;
- stupid things other people have done.

 At the time, none of this seems historically important.

 Two centuries later, it could be priceless.

 The recordings become a primary source.

 Not a grand historical document.

 Something much better:

 **a person talking normally.**

 Westminster doesn't hear "the pre-war era" described by a historian.

 He hears a man complaining about his landlord.

 He hears somebody laughing about cats.

 He hears somebody being worried about his friend.

 He hears someone getting depressed about the state of the world.

 He hears someone telling strangers to look after each other.

 He hears a human being.

 And eventually he realises:

 **That man is his ancestor.**

---

 # 5\. The music collection is another historical thread

 Tommi repeatedly refers to his grandfather's music collection.

 That gives Westminster an unexpected genealogical resource.

 The music itself becomes a chronological anchor.

 Tommi casually says things such as:

 > "This was from 2009."

 or:

 > "This band was Swedish."

 or:

 > "My granddad had this one."

 To Tommi, this is trivia.

 To Westminster, it can help reconstruct a world that has been gone for generations.

 The collection also provides a physical link between generations.

 Granddad owned the music.

 Tommi inherited it.

 Tommi played it on the radio.

 The recordings survived.

 Eventually Westminster hears it.

 The songs therefore become part of the family inheritance.

---

 # 6\. London becomes a second kind of family tree

 Tommi gives us a surprisingly detailed snapshot of his London.

 The Thames.

 Pimlico.

 Camden.

 Cheapside.

 Bethnal Green.

 The local cinema.

 The building where he lives.

 The landlord.

 The coffee shop.

 The radio station.

 His friends.

 His neighbours.

 His workplace.

 These aren't just jokes.

 They're geographical clues.

 Westminster can eventually reconstruct pieces of Tommi's life from them.

 And that means his genealogy isn't merely:

 > person → person → person → person.

 It's also:

 > **place → place → place → place.**

 The family history is rooted in actual geography.

 Westminster can walk through the ruins of the world Tommi described.

 That could be enormously powerful.

---

 # 7\. Abbey's absence is potentially significant

 One particularly interesting feature of the radio material is that Tommi doesn't appear to talk much, if at all, about Abbey.

 That omission could become useful.

 Westminster eventually discovers that Abbey is the person who carried his direct family line into Vault 81.

 But the recordings don't necessarily tell him much about her.

 Why?

 There are several possibilities.

 Perhaps Abbey was already preparing to leave.

 Perhaps Tommi and Abbey were separated.

 Perhaps the trip to America was planned.

 Perhaps Tommi expected to follow.

 Perhaps circumstances prevented it.

 Perhaps Tommi didn't know where she had gone.

 Perhaps there is another reason entirely.

 The important thing is that **the historical record has a hole in it**.

 That hole becomes a mystery.

---

 # 8\. Westminster's initial problem

 Westminster doesn't begin by thinking:

 > "I need to investigate my genealogy."

 He begins with something more personal:

 > **I don't feel right.**

 He knows something is wrong, but doesn't know what.

 He tries to explain it.

 Maybe it is:

 - genetic;
- neurological;
- psychological;
- hereditary;
- environmental;
- connected to Vault 81;
- connected to radiation;
- connected to some family history;
- connected to something his ancestors experienced.

 And because this is Fallout, all of these possibilities are sufficiently plausible that the player can't simply dismiss them.

 The genealogy investigation therefore begins as an attempt to find a **medical or biological explanation** for a deeply personal feeling.

---

 # 9\. The investigation should produce plausible wrong answers

 This is important.

 I wouldn't make the mystery a simple:

 > Everyone was wrong; here's the real answer.

 Instead, let people identify **real pieces of evidence but misunderstand what they mean**.

 A doctor might discover something unusual.

 A herbalist might identify something relevant.

 A mystic might say something that sounds ridiculous but turns out to contain a fragment of truth.

 Someone in Far Harbor might give Westminster another piece of the puzzle.

 Someone in Nuka-World might provide information that seems completely unrelated.

 Research into Vault 81 might reveal something genuinely strange about his ancestry.

 The result is that Westminster keeps thinking:

 > **That's it. That's what's wrong with me.**

 But every explanation ultimately fails to account for the whole person.

 The clues are real.

 The interpretations are incomplete.

---

 # 10\. Far Harbor can represent identity and memory

 Far Harbor is particularly useful because its themes naturally touch questions such as:

 - What makes someone who they are?
- What is the relationship between memory and identity?
- Can a person change and still be the same person?
- How much does one's history determine one's identity?
- Can someone be something other than what they were born to be?
- What does it mean to know oneself?

 Westminster can therefore encounter people who offer apparently profound explanations for him.

 Some might be correct about individual aspects.

 But none of them necessarily gives him the answer he wants.

---

 # 11\. Nuka-World can represent increasingly bizarre explanations

 Nuka-World gives you a completely different flavour.

 It's a place where Westminster can encounter:

 - pseudo-science;
- bizarre beliefs;
- opportunists;
- violence;
- spectacle;
- people confidently claiming to understand things they don't understand.

 Someone can tell him:

 > "Oh, you want answers about your weird hereditary condition? You need to talk to X."

 So he does.

 And X gives him an absolutely bonkers explanation.

 And, crucially, it sounds convincing.

 For a while.

 This can become a recurring structure:

 **Westminster finds an explanation → believes it → discovers it doesn't quite fit → continues searching.**

---

 # 12\. The final mystic should probably be right in the most irritating way possible

 The rough idea you already have is:

 > "No, it's none of that. You need to look deep inside yourself and you will know what it is. When you know that, you'll need to go to XXX, and that will make you right."

 I think this can work extremely well.

 The mystic isn't necessarily diagnosing Westminster with supernatural insight.

 They're telling him:

 > **You've spent this entire journey looking for an external explanation for an internal problem.**

 Westminster doesn't want to hear that.

 Because he has spent the entire story looking for:

 **genes → disease → ancestry → Vault → radiation → brain → mutation → family history → curse → explanation.**

 And the final answer is essentially:

 > **You already know what is wrong. You just don't want to admit it.**

 That turns the whole investigation inward.

---

 # 13\. The genealogy is therefore a metaphor

 Westminster thinks he is investigating:

 > **Where did I come from?**

 But underneath that, he is actually asking:

 > **Who am I?**

 Those are different questions.

 He can reconstruct six generations of people and still not understand himself.

 He can discover exactly where his family came from and still be faced with the same fundamental problem.

 The genealogy gives him history.

 It doesn't automatically give him identity.

 That distinction could be central to the story.

---

 # 14\. The final destination should be about action, not information

 The missing **XXX** is therefore important.

 I wouldn't make it simply:

 > "the place where Westminster learns the answer."

 By that point, he should already know the answer.

 Instead:

 > **XXX should be the place where Westminster has to act on what he knows.**

 The investigation is about knowledge.

 The ending is about choice.

 That gives you a very clean structure:

 **Tommi**\
 → ordinary man before the apocalypse\
 → Abbey and baby leave for America\
 → Vault 81\
 → generations pass\
 → Westminster is born\
 → Westminster feels that something is wrong\
 → he searches for a physical/genetic explanation\
 → investigates increasingly strange corners of the wasteland\
 → reconstructs his ancestry\
 → discovers the history\
 → realises history isn't the answer\
 → understands himself\
 → goes to XXX\
 → makes the choice that resolves the story.

---

 # 15\. Tommi and Westminster are thematic opposites

 This may be the most important piece.

 Tommi's philosophy throughout the radio material is essentially:

 > **The world is fucked. Be decent anyway.**

 He complains constantly.

 He gets drunk.

 He makes mistakes.

 He gets depressed.

 He behaves irresponsibly.

 He is sometimes a complete idiot.

 But underneath all of it, he repeatedly returns to the same idea:

 **look after people.**

 He says things like:

 > "If we just look out for each other a bit..."

 and:

 > "We could all do a tiny bit more than the bare minimum..."

 and:

 > "I didn't make anyone's day worse. I did OK."

 That's his moral centre.

 And Westminster, as his descendant, may inherit something more important than DNA.

 He inherits a **way of looking at the world**.

 Tommi doesn't know what the future will bring.

 He doesn't know that his world is about to end.

 He doesn't know his child will survive.

 He doesn't know his descendants will eventually emerge from Vault 81.

 He simply tries, imperfectly, to be decent.

 That can become Westminster's inheritance.

---

 # 16\. The radio show can become Westminster's "ghost"

 Imagine Westminster finally obtaining the recordings.

 He's spent the story reconstructing:

 > Abbey Westminster\
>  ↓\
>  A\
>  ↓\
>  B\
>  ↓\
>  C\
>  ↓\
>  D\
>  ↓\
>  E\
>  ↓\
>  Westminster

 Until then, these people are mostly names.

 Then he hears Tommi's voice.

 And Tommi isn't a historical legend.

 He's this ridiculous man talking about:

 - cats;
- his landlord;
- his clothes;
- his friends;
- coffee;
- music;
- getting drunk;
- the weather;
- his miserable life;
- trying to be decent.

 Westminster expected history.

 Instead, he finds a person.

 That could be a very powerful moment.

---

 # 17\. The trans-representation dimension

 This is an area where tone will matter enormously.

 The strongest version, I think, is **not** to make Westminster's identity into a puzzle that the narrative eventually "solves".

 If Westminster is trans, then the story needs to distinguish between:

 > **"There is something wrong with me."**

 and:

 > **"There is something I don't yet understand about myself."**

 Those are not the same thing.

 The former is a potentially dangerous framing if the story ultimately confirms that being trans was the disease/curse/mutation/genetic problem all along.

 The latter gives you much more room.

 Westminster can genuinely feel alienated from himself.

 He can genuinely search for explanations.

 He can make mistakes about what those feelings mean.

 He can listen to people who give him bad explanations.

 He can be frightened by what he discovers.

 He can investigate his family history.

 And eventually he can arrive at a deeper understanding of himself.

 **The fact that he was wrong about the cause of his discomfort does not make the discomfort fake.**

 That distinction is important.

 Likewise, the story doesn't need to turn the eventual revelation into a clinical diagnosis.

 It can be something closer to recognition.

 A moment of:

 > _Oh._

 Not necessarily:

 > _I have discovered the thing that is wrong with me._

 But:

 > _I finally understand what I've been trying to tell myself._

 That leaves Westminster's transness as part of his personhood rather than making it a supernatural plot twist.

---

 # 18\. Tommi's philosophy can support that without him being a perfect ally

 Another potentially nice thing is that Tommi doesn't need to be a flawless enlightened ancestor.

 He lived in 2077.

 He's a messy guy.

 He drinks too much.

 He says stupid things.

 He makes inappropriate jokes.

 He gets things wrong.

 But he has a fundamentally decent instinct:

 **people deserve to be allowed to be themselves, and people should look after each other.**

 That makes him human rather than a mouthpiece.

 And Westminster doesn't need to inherit a perfectly articulated philosophy from him.

 He can inherit fragments.

 The radio recordings are full of them.

---

 # 19\. The story's central inheritance

 There are therefore several different things being inherited through the family:

 ### Biological inheritance

 Whatever Westminster's actual biological situation is.

 ### Historical inheritance

 The knowledge of what happened to his ancestors.

 ### Cultural inheritance

 The music, stories, habits and memories passed down through Vault 81.

 ### Emotional inheritance

 The unresolved questions and absences left by previous generations.

 ### Moral inheritance

 Tommi's basic philosophy:

 > **The world is awful. Try not to make it worse.**

 And finally:

 ### Personal inheritance

 The thing Westminster chooses to do with all of this.

 That last one cannot be inherited.

 He has to choose it himself.

---

 # 20\. The emotional shape

 The overall progression could therefore be:

 ### Act I — Something is wrong

 Westminster feels wrong.

 He doesn't understand why.

 He searches for a medical explanation.

 ### Act II — Something happened before me

 His investigation leads into genealogy.

 He discovers Vault 81.

 He discovers Abbey.

 He discovers the generations between Abbey and himself.

 He starts reconstructing Tommi's life.

 ### Act III — Everything means something

 He investigates further.

 Far Harbor.

 Nuka-World.

 Doctors.

 Mystics.

 Herbalists.

 Fortune tellers.

 Old records.

 Family stories.

 Everywhere he finds another explanation.

 Every explanation contains a fragment of truth.

 None explains him.

 ### Act IV — The wrong question

 Eventually someone tells him:

 > **You're asking the wrong question.**

 He has been asking:

 > "What happened to me?"

 when the question he actually needs to ask is:

 > **"Who am I?"**

 ### Act V — Recognition

 He finally understands himself.

 Not necessarily because somebody tells him.

 Because he recognises it.

 ### Act VI — XXX

 The mystic's final instruction sends him somewhere.

 This isn't another research trip.

 It's an action.

 He knows what he has to do.

 He goes.

 And for once, he isn't looking for somebody else to tell him who he is.

---

 # 21\. The title connection

 There is also something lovely about **As the World Burns** as the Tommi side of the story.

 Tommi is literally broadcasting while the world is approaching its end.

 But he doesn't know that.

 His show is full of petty complaints, jokes, music and ordinary human interaction.

 The world is about to burn.

 And Tommi is worrying about:

 > "Why has my landlord installed vending machines?"

 That's funny.

 But it's also poignant.

 Because that's what the end of the world actually looks like from inside a person's life.

 People don't spend the final months walking around announcing:

 > "I am now living through history."

 They still go to work.

 They still complain about rent.

 They still listen to music.

 They still fall in love.

 They still argue with their friends.

 They still worry about cats.

 They still get drunk.

 They still try to be decent.

 **The world burns around them while life continues.**

 And 210 years later, Westminster is still living inside the consequences.

---

 # 22\. The deepest thematic statement

 If I had to reduce the whole thing to one idea, I think it would be:

 > **You don't get to choose the world you inherit, or the history that made you. You do get to choose what you do with yourself once you understand it.**

 Tommi cannot save the old world.

 Abbey cannot preserve the old world.

 Vault 81 cannot preserve everything.

 Westminster cannot change his ancestry.

 But Westminster can decide what the inheritance means.

 And that makes the ending about agency rather than destiny.

---

 # 23\. The really lovely symmetry

 Tommi spends his final broadcasts saying, in one form or another:

 > **Everything is fucked. Anyway, here's some music.**

 Westminster begins his story with the opposite attitude:

 > **Something is wrong with me. I need to find out what it is.**

 By the end, he can arrive somewhere between those two ideas:

 > **The world is fucked. I know who I am. Now what am I going to do?**

 That's the point where Tommi's inheritance becomes Westminster's own.

 Not his DNA.

 Not his history.

 Not his grandfather's records.

 Not the Vault.

 Not some mystical prophecy.

 **His choice.**

 And I think that is where the story has the potential to become something considerably more interesting than simply a Fallout ancestry mystery.

 It's a story about a person trying to find the explanation for himself, discovering his entire history in the process, and eventually realising that knowing where he came from is only useful if it helps him decide where he's going.

 And genuinely, I think you've got a very solid foundation here. The radio material gives you an unusually rich way of making the pre-war world feel _lived in_ rather than merely "the world before Fallout", while Westminster gives you a reason to revisit all of it from a completely different emotional perspective.

 The representation question is something I'd keep treating with exactly the care you're describing: **don't make Westminster's identity the pathology the plot is trying to diagnose.** Let him have a real, difficult experience of not understanding himself, let the world around him offer plenty of explanations, and let his eventual self-understanding belong to him. That leaves you enormous room for ambiguity, humour, weirdness and Fallout-style bullshit without making the character's identity itself the thing that needs fixing.
