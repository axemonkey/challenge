 # WESTMINSTER / AS THE WORLD BURNS

 ## 1\. Core Premise

 **Westminster's story takes place in 2287, when he is 40 years old.**

 He has always felt that something about himself isn't quite right. He cannot properly articulate what the problem is, but the feeling is persistent enough that he begins searching for an explanation.

 At first, he assumes the answer must be something external:

 - a genetic condition;
- a hereditary illness;
- neurological damage;
- some strange consequence of Vault 81;
- radiation;
- something that happened to one of his ancestors;
- something buried in his family's history.

 His search gradually becomes an investigation into his ancestry.

 That investigation eventually takes him across the wasteland, including places such as Far Harbor and Nuka-World, and brings him into contact with doctors, herbalists, mystics, fortune tellers and various people who claim to understand what is happening to him.

 Each explanation seems plausible for a while.

 None of them is quite right.

 Eventually Westminster is forced to confront the possibility that he has been asking the wrong question.

 The story ultimately becomes less about discovering **what is wrong with him** and more about discovering **who he is**.

---

 # 2\. The Two Stories

 There are really two intertwined stories.

 ## Tommi Starr — 2077

 Tommi is a pirate-radio DJ broadcasting **"As the World Burns"** from London shortly before the Great War.

 His broadcasts are funny, rambling, occasionally melancholy and often ridiculous.

 He talks about:

 - music;
- his friends;
- his flat;
- his neighbours;
- coffee;
- drinking;
- work;
- cats;
- London;
- the state of the world;
- stupid things he's done;
- things he doesn't understand;
- trying to be a decent person.

 He has absolutely no idea that the world is about to end.

 He certainly has no idea that his voice will eventually become a historical artefact listened to by one of his descendants.

 ## Westminster — 2287

 Two centuries later, Westminster is living in the consequences of the world Tommi inhabited.

 He initially knows almost nothing about Tommi.

 As his investigation develops, he reconstructs his family history and eventually discovers that the ridiculous man whose radio broadcasts he has been hearing is one of his ancestors.

 The recordings therefore become something more than historical evidence.

 They become a connection to a person.

### Important correction

 Westminster is **40 in 2287**.

 This is now the canonical version.

 His age is useful dramatically: he is not a teenager or young adult having his first existential crisis. He is a fully grown man who has already lived a considerable amount of his life before finally deciding that he cannot ignore the feeling any longer.

---

 # 4\. ESTABLISHED: Abbey and Child A

 Abbey Westminster gives birth to Child A in March 2077.

 By October, she and the seven-month-old child have somehow reached Vault 81.

 The precise circumstances are currently unresolved.

 This is potentially one of the major mysteries of the family history.

 The striking image is simple:

 **March 2077:** Tommi and Abbey have a baby.

 **October 2077:** the world ends.

 Abbey is separated from Tommi and somehow gets the baby into a Vault.

 From that point onward, the family becomes part of Vault 81's closed community.

 Over the following generations, the family history becomes increasingly detached from the world outside.

---

 # 5\. TO DECIDE: Tommi's Fate

 Tommi's fate should probably remain unresolved for some time.

 The current possibility is that he dies in London during the Great War.

 But this does not necessarily need to be established immediately.

 Potentially, Westminster initially assumes that Tommi died.

 Later evidence could complicate that assumption.

 The important thing is that Tommi does not necessarily need to become some secret pre-war superhero.

 There is something more interesting about the possibility that he remains exactly what he appears to be:

 **a funny, flawed, ordinary man caught in an extraordinary historical event.**

 His significance comes not from becoming legendary, but from being human.

---

 # 6\. ESTABLISHED: The Radio Broadcasts

 Tommi's broadcasts are a major part of the story.

 They should not simply function as comedy sketches or exposition about the pre-war world.

 They are **primary-source historical material**.

 Westminster eventually gets to hear a person from 2077 talking normally.

 Not a historian.

 Not a politician.

 Not a military commander.

 Not somebody explaining the apocalypse.

 Just a bloke talking rubbish on the radio.

 He complains about his life.

 He recommends music.

 He talks about his friends.

 He drinks too much.

 He gets depressed.

 He says stupid things.

 He occasionally says something unexpectedly profound.

 He worries about whether he's being a decent person.

 And all the while, the audience knows what Tommi does not:

 **the world is about to end.**

 That creates the fundamental dramatic irony of the radio sections.

---

 # 7\. Tommi's World

 The broadcasts establish an unusually ordinary picture of pre-war London.

 We hear about:

 - London itself;
- Tommi's building;
- his flatmate;
- his landlord;
- Camden;
- Cheapside;
- Bethnal Green;
- the Thames;
- local bars;
- coffee shops;
- workplaces;
- friends and neighbours;
- the pirate station C-ROK;
- everyday annoyances;
- ordinary nightlife.

 This gives Westminster something to reconstruct later.

 The geography becomes part of the genealogy.

 His family tree isn't simply:

 **person → person → person → person**

 It is also:

 **place → place → place → place.**

 Westminster can potentially walk through or investigate places Tommi casually mentioned centuries earlier.

---

 # 8\. The Music Collection

 Tommi repeatedly references his granddad's music collection.

 This provides another thread through time.

 The music collection is both:

 **a source of comedy in Tommi's story**

 and

 **an historical artefact in Westminster's story.**

 Tommi treats the records as a convenient source of music.

 Westminster can eventually recognise them as evidence of a vanished culture.

 The songs also give Tommi's sections their own identity.

 They are not merely background music.

 They become the soundtrack of the final days of his world.

---

 # 9\. Abbey's Absence

 One potentially significant feature of Tommi's broadcasts is that Abbey does not appear to be a major presence in them.

 That creates a gap.

 Westminster knows that Abbey is enormously important to his family history.

 But Tommi's recordings may tell him relatively little about her.

 This can become one of the things Westminster has to investigate.

 Possible explanations include:

 - Abbey was already preparing to leave;
- Tommi and Abbey were separated;
- the trip to America had been planned;
- Tommi intended to follow;
- circumstances prevented him from doing so;
- Tommi did not know where Abbey went;
- something else entirely.

 **The absence of information is itself part of the mystery.**

---

 # 10\. ESTABLISHED: Westminster's Initial Problem

 Westminster does not begin his story thinking:

 > "I need to investigate my genealogy."

 He begins with:

 > **"Something isn't right."**

 He does not necessarily have a precise symptom.

 It is more existential than that.

 He simply doesn't feel properly aligned with himself.

 Because this is the Fallout universe, there are plenty of plausible explanations.

 Maybe he's sick.

 Maybe something is wrong with his brain.

 Maybe his genes are unusual.

 Maybe something happened to his ancestors.

 Maybe Vault 81 has something to do with it.

 Maybe the wasteland has affected him somehow.

 Maybe someone knows something.

 So he starts looking.

---

 # 11\. THE INVESTIGATION

 Westminster's journey can take him through a succession of possible explanations.

 Potential sources include:

 - doctors;
- herbalists;
- mystics;
- fortune tellers;
- genealogical records;
- Vault 81 records;
- family stories;
- surviving pre-war material;
- Far Harbor;
- Nuka-World;
- other people with strange experiences;
- people who confidently claim to know what is happening.

 The important structural principle is:

 **the clues can be real even when the conclusions are wrong.**

 Someone may genuinely identify something unusual about Westminster.

 Someone else may correctly identify an event in his family history.

 Someone else may tell him something metaphorically useful.

 But each person interprets the evidence differently.

 Westminster repeatedly thinks:

 > **That's it. I've found the answer.**

 Then discovers that it doesn't quite explain everything.

---

 # 12\. Far Harbor

 Far Harbor is particularly useful thematically because its existing material already deals heavily with questions of:

 - identity;
- memory;
- personhood;
- history;
- transformation;
- self-perception;
- what makes somebody the person they are.

 Westminster can encounter people there who have very strong opinions about identity and what constitutes a person.

 Some of what they tell him may be useful.

 Some may be wrong.

 Some may be disturbing.

 The important thing is that Far Harbor does not need to provide **the answer**.

 It can provide another set of questions.

---

 # 13\. Nuka-World

 Nuka-World provides a different flavour.

 Where Far Harbor can be philosophical and introspective, Nuka-World can be stranger, more exploitative and more absurd.

 Westminster can encounter:

 - pseudo-science;
- strange beliefs;
- opportunists;
- people selling certainty;
- people who claim to understand mysteries they plainly do not understand.

 This is useful because Westminster is desperate enough to listen.

 He has a question.

 Someone offers an answer.

 He follows it.

 And for a while, he believes it.

 Then it falls apart.

---

 # 14\. The Wrong Answers

 The story works best if Westminster does not simply encounter a series of obviously stupid people.

 Instead, let the explanations be **plausible**.

 Perhaps there really is something medically unusual about him.

 Perhaps there really is an unusual piece of family history.

 Perhaps there really is some unexplained event in Vault 81.

 Perhaps someone really does know something about his ancestry.

 But none of those facts necessarily explains the central problem.

 The mystery therefore becomes increasingly frustrating.

 Westminster is accumulating information.

 But information is not necessarily understanding.

---

 # 15\. The Mystic

 Eventually Westminster returns to a mystic-type character.

 The mystic essentially tells him:

 > **No. It's none of that.**

 Westminster has spent the entire journey looking outward.

 The mystic tells him to look inward.

 The essential idea is:

 **You already know.**

 And then:

 **Once you understand it, go to XXX.**

 This should be irritating rather than comforting.

 Westminster has travelled enormous distances.

 He has investigated his ancestry.

 He has searched through history.

 He has consulted experts.

 He has chased ridiculous theories.

 And the final answer is basically:

 > **You've been asking the wrong question.**

---

 # 16\. The Central Question

 Westminster thinks he is asking:

 > **"What happened to me?"**

 But the real question is:

 > **"Who am I?"**

 Those are fundamentally different.

 His genealogy can tell him where he came from.

 It cannot automatically tell him who he is.

 His medical records can tell him things about his body.

 They cannot tell him what he should do with his life.

 His ancestors can explain some of his history.

 They cannot choose his future.

 This is the point at which the genealogy stops being merely a mystery and becomes a metaphor.

---

 # 17\. The Genealogy as Metaphor

 Westminster spends the story going backwards.

 He follows:

 **Westminster**\
 ↓\
 **E**\
 ↓\
 **D**\
 ↓\
 **C**\
 ↓\
 **B**\
 ↓\
 **A**\
 ↓\
 **Abbey**\
 ↓\
 **Tommi / 2077**

 But eventually he has to stop going backwards.

 He has to turn around.

 The family tree answers:

 > **Where did I come from?**

 The story ultimately asks:

 > **Where am I going?**

---

 # 18\. Tommi's Moral Philosophy

 One of the strongest things in the radio material is Tommi's recurring philosophy.

 It isn't sophisticated.

 It isn't presented as wisdom.

 It is essentially:

 > **The world is fucked. Try not to make it worse.**

 Tommi says, in various forms, that if he can get through a day without making somebody else's life worse, then he has done all right.

 He believes people should do a little more than the bare minimum.

 He recognises that selfishness and indifference contribute to the state of the world.

 He isn't perfect.

 He drinks.

 He makes mistakes.

 He behaves irresponsibly.

 He says stupid things.

 But there is a basic decency underneath him.

 That is important.

---

 # 19\. Tommi and Westminster

 Tommi and Westminster can therefore function as thematic opposites.

 Tommi lives immediately before the apocalypse.

 He does not know what is coming.

 His response to a terrible world is essentially:

 > **Fuck it. Here's some music.**

 Westminster lives in the aftermath.

 His response is:

 > **Something is wrong. I need to understand why.**

 Tommi looks at the world and tries to live in it.

 Westminster looks at himself and tries to explain himself.

 Eventually Westminster has to arrive at something like Tommi's position:

 > **The world is fucked. I know who I am. What am I going to do now?**

---

 # 20\. The Radio as a Ghost

 There is potentially a very strong emotional moment when Westminster finally hears Tommi.

 Until then, his ancestors are names.

 A.

 B.

 C.

 D.

 E.

 Abbey.

 Tommi.

 Then suddenly:

 **Tommi has a voice.**

 He isn't a historical abstraction.

 He's alive in the recording.

 He's talking about stupid things.

 He's complaining.

 He's laughing.

 He's playing music.

 He's being an idiot.

 Westminster expected history.

 Instead, he finds a person.

 That distinction could be emotionally enormous.

 # 21\. The Inheritance

 Westminster inherits several different things from his ancestors.

 ## Biological inheritance

 Whatever physical/genetic characteristics actually pass through the family.

 ## Historical inheritance

 The knowledge of what happened to the people before him.

 ## Cultural inheritance

 Music, stories, habits and traditions preserved through Vault 81.

 ## Emotional inheritance

 Unresolved questions and absences.

 ## Moral inheritance

 Tommi's basic philosophy:

 > **Try not to make the world worse.**

 ## Personal inheritance

 The one thing nobody can give Westminster.

 **What he chooses to do with everything he has inherited.**

 # 22\. TRANS REPRESENTATION — IMPORTANT WRITING PRINCIPLE

 If Westminster is trans, the story needs to be careful about the distinction between:

 > **"There is something wrong with me."**

 and:

 > **"There is something about myself I don't yet understand."**

 These are not the same thing.

 The dangerous version of the story would ultimately reveal:

 > Westminster was trans, therefore something was biologically wrong with him.

 That would make his identity itself the pathology.

 The stronger version is:

 > Westminster genuinely feels that something is wrong, but he is mistaken about what that feeling means.

 His discomfort is real.

 His confusion is real.

 His search is real.

 The explanations he encounters can be sincere and sometimes useful.

 But being trans does not need to become the disease, mutation, curse or hereditary defect that the entire plot has been trying to diagnose.

 His eventual understanding can instead be an act of recognition.

 Not:

 > **"I have discovered what's wrong with me."**

 But:

 > **"I finally understand what I've been trying to tell myself."**

 That distinction gives the character considerably more room to be a whole person.

---

 # 23\. Westminster Does Not Need to Have Everything Figured Out Immediately

 He does not need to emerge from the revelation with perfect certainty.

 The story does not have to imply that one mystical conversation magically resolves every aspect of his identity.

 The important change can simply be that he finally understands the direction in which he needs to go.

 The mystic doesn't necessarily give him an identity.

 They give him permission to stop looking for one in the wrong places.

---

 # 24\. Tommi Does Not Need to Be a Perfect Ancestor

 Tommi should remain a messy human being.

 He can be:

 - funny;
- irresponsible;
- selfish sometimes;
- generous sometimes;
- drunk;
- frightened;
- horny;
- depressed;
- kind;
- irritating;
- genuinely thoughtful.

 He doesn't need to become a flawless enlightened ancestor who somehow anticipated Westminster's entire life.

 His value is precisely that he is imperfect.

 Westminster isn't inheriting a set of instructions.

 He's inheriting fragments of a human life.

 # 25\. The Story Structure

 ## ACT I — SOMETHING IS WRONG

 Westminster, 40, lives in 2287.

 He knows something isn't right.

 He begins looking for an explanation.

 The initial possibilities are predominantly physical or hereditary.

---

 ## ACT II — SOMETHING HAPPENED BEFORE ME

 The investigation leads toward ancestry.

 Westminster discovers the connection to Vault 81.

 He reconstructs the family line.

 Eventually he discovers Abbey.

 Eventually he discovers Tommi.

 The past starts becoming tangible.

---

 ## ACT III — EVERYTHING MEANS SOMETHING

 Westminster investigates further.

 He travels.

 He consults people.

 He visits strange places.

 Far Harbor.

 Nuka-World.

 Doctors.

 Mystics.

 Herbalists.

 Records.

 People who claim to know things.

 Everywhere he finds another possible explanation.

---

 ## ACT IV — THE WRONG QUESTION

 Eventually somebody forces Westminster to confront the fact that all his explanations have something in common.

 They are all external.

 He is looking for something to **blame**.

 Genes.

 Radiation.

 Vault 81.

 His ancestors.

 History.

 Disease.

 The apocalypse.

 Anything except himself.

---

 ## ACT V — RECOGNITION

 Westminster finally understands something fundamental about himself.

 The precise nature and presentation of this revelation are still to be developed.

 The important thing is that the answer comes through **recognition**, not diagnosis.

---

 ## ACT VI — XXX

 The mystic has told him:

 > Once you know, go to XXX.

 Now Westminster knows.

 So he goes.

 This should not simply be another place where he receives information.

 It should be a place where he has to **act**.

 The investigation has been about knowledge.

 The ending should be about choice.

---

 # 26\. TO DECIDE: What Is XXX?

 The final location is currently one of the biggest open questions.

 The ideal destination should do more than provide another clue.

 It should require Westminster to make a meaningful choice.

 Possible functions for XXX include:

 - confronting somebody;
- protecting somebody;
- returning somewhere;
- leaving somewhere;
- claiming something;

 The destination should preferably make the thematic point:

 > **Understanding yourself is not the end of the story. What you do with that understanding is.**

---

 # 27\. The Title: AS THE WORLD BURNS

 The title becomes increasingly appropriate when considered from Tommi's perspective.

 Tommi is broadcasting while the world is approaching its end.

 He doesn't know that.

 He's still worrying about:

 - his clothes;
- coffee;
- music;
- his landlord;
- his friends;
- getting home;
- being tired;
- drinking too much;
- whether listeners are enjoying the show.

 The apocalypse doesn't announce itself as "the apocalypse" to every person experiencing it.

 People still have ordinary lives.

 They still go to work.

 They still argue.

 They still fall in love.

 They still get drunk.

 They still listen to music.

 They still worry about stupid things.

 **The world burns while life continues.**

---

 # 28\. The Deepest Thematic Statement

 The story can ultimately be understood as:

 > **You don't get to choose the world you inherit, or the history that made you. You do get to choose what you do with yourself once you understand it.**

 Tommi cannot save the old world.

 Abbey cannot preserve the old world.

 Vault 81 cannot preserve everything.

 Westminster cannot change his ancestry.

 But Westminster can decide what his inheritance means.

 That makes the ending about **agency rather than destiny**.

---

 # 29. The Central Symmetry

 Tommi's philosophy:

 > **Everything is fucked. Anyway, here's some music.**

 Westminster's starting point:

 > **Something is wrong with me. I need to find out what it is.**

 Westminster's eventual position:

 > **The world is fucked. I know who I am. Now what am I going to do?**

 That is where Tommi's inheritance becomes Westminster's own.

 Not his DNA.

 Not his family tree.

 Not the Vault.

 Not his grandfather's records.

 Not a prophecy.

 **His choice.**

 # 32\. The Guiding Principle

 When developing the story, the most useful question may be:

 > **Is this helping Westminster understand himself, or is it merely giving him another explanation for why he thinks he's broken?**

 If it is the latter, that isn't necessarily a problem.

 It may be exactly what the story needs.

 Because Westminster's journey isn't ultimately about finding the correct diagnosis.

 It's about discovering that he has spent years trying to explain his existence to himself as though he were a problem that somebody else needed to solve.

 Eventually, he has to stop asking:

 > **"What's wrong with me?"**

 and start asking:

 > **"What do I want to do with the person I am?"**

 That is where the story belongs.
